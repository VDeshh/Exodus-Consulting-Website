How it actually runs

Before anything is built, this is the week we are describing.

No software can be scoped from a category name. These are the specifics of nightlife and hospitality that decide what is worth building and what is not.

  1. 01

    The busiest night is the least measured

    Saturday makes the month, and Saturday is when nobody has a free hand to write anything down. The door is three deep, a table wants another bottle, and a bartender has walked off shift. Decisions get made and never recorded. On Monday the owner has a POS total, a bank deposit and a group chat, and no way to explain the gap between them.

  2. 02

    The floor plan lives in three places

    The host holds the real version on their phone, a promoter holds last week's version, and the sheet at the door is older than both. So a table gets promised twice and the argument happens in front of the guest who paid for it. The room is the most valuable inventory a venue owns, and it is usually the only inventory nobody counts properly.

  3. 03

    The bar count happens at 3am with a clipboard

    Someone tired walks every bar, eyeballs the level in each bottle, and writes tenths on a sheet. It takes an hour, it is done differently by whoever got stuck with it, and the numbers land in a spreadsheet a week later. By then the night it describes is history, and nobody can tie a missing bottle back to a shift or a person.

  4. 04

    Promoters get paid on names, not spend

    A promoter brings forty people and invoices for forty people. Whether those forty bought anything at the bar is not in the number, because the guestlist and the till have never met. Every venue has the promoter everyone knows fills the room, and the promoter everyone suspects does not. The difference is real money and it has never been written down.

  5. 05

    The ticket buyer belongs to the ticketing site

    A guest buys through Eventbrite or Ticketmaster, comes to the room, spends three hundred dollars and disappears. The venue has the sale and not the person. Next month the same event goes out to whoever sits on an old mailing list, and the people who already proved they will show up and spend are not on that list.

  6. 06

    Table minimums are set by feel

    The minimum on the best table is whatever it was last year, plus a number the host invents when a large party asks on the night. Nobody compares what the table actually cleared against what the minimum said, or against what a different party would have cleared in the same four hours. The best asset in the room is priced from memory.

What we build

8 builds that pay for themselves here.

Each one states what it reads, what it does, and what a person still approves. Scope is agreed after the audit, one build at a time, against a baseline you sign.

Pour reconciled to sales, per bartender

Every menu item carries a recipe of inventory items: a vodka soda is one ounce of vodka and a measured pour of soda. POS sales times recipes gives theoretical usage for the night, and the close count gives actual. The gap is reported in ounces and in dollars, and because every terminal maps to the employee working it, the number ties to a bartender. The owner reads it first.

Measured by: Variance in dollars per bar, per night, and per bartender.

Bluetooth scale weigh-in at close

Open bottles get weighed instead of eyeballed. The scales connect over Bluetooth and each weight lands in the count as it is read, so a full bar takes minutes rather than a clipboard hour. Tare weights and pour specs are set once per product. A manager signs the session off before it becomes the night's actual usage, and the session stays on the record afterwards.

Measured by: Minutes to complete a full close count, and how many nights it actually gets done.

One guest record from every ticket seller

The system connects to the marketplaces the venue already sells on and links each event to its outside listing. Buyer name, email, phone and birthday sync into the venue's own guest records, tickets become passes, and the door scanner accepts both. Nothing is migrated and no contract is broken. The owner picks which events are linked and which stay separate.

Measured by: Share of ticket buyers who exist as a guest record the venue owns.

A voice agent on the venue line

The agent answers the phone and clears the calls that repeat: hours, age policy, dress code, what the table minimum is, what is on this Friday, whether a booking exists. It reads the live event and table records rather than a script written last season. Anything it cannot resolve transfers to a person with the caller's context attached, so the guest is not asked to start again.

Measured by: Share of calls resolved without a person, and calls answered outside office hours.

A chatbot on the event page

The chatbot sits on the venue's own event pages and answers what fills the inbox: what a table package includes, what the minimum is, whether the guestlist is still open, how bottle service works, where to park. It quotes the live listing rather than a PDF from last season. Booking and guestlist requests are handed to a host to confirm, not confirmed by the bot.

Measured by: Inbound emails and direct messages per event week.

Campaigns drafted from your own spend data

The operator describes the night they want to fill, and the system drafts the campaign, the offer or the automated email, SMS and WhatsApp flow, using reference material we assembled from five years in nightlife. The audience is built from real attendance and spend: who came to which kind of night, what they spent, when they lapsed. A person edits it and a person sends it.

Measured by: Revenue per send, and spend per guest brought back.

Loyalty that follows the guest between rooms

Tiers, points and perks are configured once, and passes issue to Apple and Google Wallet. A tier earned in one room is recognised at the next, so a group of venues stops competing for the same person's first visit and starts sharing their fifth. Spend from the POS and the guest profile decides the tier. Perks that cost money need an owner to approve them.

Measured by: Repeat visit rate within ninety days.

A night report that assembles itself

By the time the owner wakes up the night is counted: revenue by bar, attendance against capacity, table turnover, promoter and host performance, comps, voids and the pour variance. Every figure names the record it came from, so a number that looks wrong can be opened rather than argued about. Nothing is estimated to fill a gap, and a missing feed is shown as missing.

Measured by: Hours a week spent building the Monday numbers by hand.

Where the data comes from

Your systems of record stay exactly where they are.

We read them, we do not replace them. Each one below says what we connect to, how, and the line the build does not cross.

Point of sale and payments

Usually: Square, Toast, Lightspeed, TouchBistro, Clover

What it holds. Every item, price, modifier and tax code, plus each tab, void, comp, tip and card settlement, by terminal and by timestamp.

How we connect. API in both directions. Menus, prices and packages push out to the terminals, and closed tabs pull back attached to a table, an event and a bar. Fennec runs this in production with Square, Toast and Lightspeed, including tab to table matching.

Where it stops. No field is written back until one system is named the owner of that field in writing. Refunds, voids and comps stay in the hands of a manager on the terminal, and we never create or delete a button mid service.

Ticketing, door and guestlist

Usually: Eventbrite, Ticketmaster, Showpass, Tixr, TicketWeb, POSH, Ora, Flite City

What it holds. Buyer name, email, phone and birthday, order value, ticket type, promo code and scan history for every event.

How we connect. Connect the account once, then link each event to its outside listing. Buyers sync into the venue's own guest records, tickets become Fennec Passes, and the door scanner reads either the pass or the original ticket, offline if the venue wifi drops at 11pm.

Where it stops. We do not change pricing, allocations or refunds on the marketplace. The venue keeps selling where it already sells and the marketplace stays the seller of record. Which events are linked is the owner's call, event by event.

Reservations, guest records and marketing

Usually: SevenRooms, OpenTable, Tock, Mailchimp, Klaviyo, Laylo, Meta Ads Manager

What it holds. Bookings, deposits, no show history, consent status, campaign sends and opens, ad spend and attributed sales.

How we connect. API or scheduled export into one guest profile carrying lifetime spend, visit count, preferences and consent. Consented guests mirror to Laylo, with RSVPs and opt outs matched back, and server side conversions send outside ticket sales back to the campaign that caused them.

Where it stops. Consent is never inferred from a purchase and never carried across venues without it. Nothing sends until a person presses the button, and an unsubscribe is honoured everywhere within one profile rather than per tool.

Stock, staff and the back office

Usually: Sculpture Hospitality, WISK, BevSpot, MarketMan, 7shifts, Push Operations, QuickBooks Online, Xero

What it holds. The product catalogue, supplier invoices and purchase orders, counts per site, rotas, clock in records and the general ledger.

How we connect. Scheduled export or a read only replica, folded into one catalogue across sites so purchased against sold becomes a subtraction rather than an argument. Counts captured on the floor reconcile against POS sales for the same period, per bar.

Where it stops. We post no journal entries and we pay nobody. Payroll, remittances and filings stay with the bookkeeper. A count is never adjusted after the fact to make a variance report look better, and the original session stays on the record.

Built around your rules

The regimes that govern this work, and how the build answers each one.

Constraints come first, because they decide the architecture. Bring us your hosting, residency and regulatory rules at the start and we design to them rather than around them.

Regulatory and professional obligations that shape a build in nightlife and hospitality.
Regime What it demands here How the build complies
PIPEDA Federal privacy law over commercial handling of personal information: collect for a stated purpose, keep only what you need, protect it, and let a guest see what you hold. One guest profile with a purpose recorded per field, retention set per category, and access limited by role so a door scanner sees a name and a status rather than lifetime spend.
BC Personal Information Protection Act, with Alberta PIPA and Quebec Law 25 where you operate Provincial privacy rules for private sector businesses, including consent, access requests and, under Law 25, a named privacy officer and notice when data crosses a border. The venue's records stay the venue's records, hosting location is agreed before the build, and an access or deletion request runs from one profile instead of six tools that each hold a copy.
CASL Consent before a commercial email, SMS or message, a working unsubscribe honoured within ten business days, and sender identification on every send. Consent is stored on the guest profile with its source and date, an unsubscribe applies across every channel at once, and a send blocks if the audience contains anyone without a recorded basis.
PCI DSS Card data has to stay inside the payment environment: terminals, tokenisation, no card numbers in your own tables or in a text message. Cards are handled by the POS and payment provider. We hold a token and a reference, never a card number, and deposits and table fees route through the processor already on the counter.
Provincial liquor licensing and server certification BC's Liquor Control and Licensing Act, the AGCO's rules in Ontario and their equivalents set service hours, capacity against occupant load, and require certified servers such as Serving It Right or Smart Serve. Capacity is a hard limit on the floor plan and the door count, service hours are set per event, and certification expiry is tracked against the rota so an uncertified server cannot be scheduled onto a bar.

Attendance and room capacity per event come out of the same records the door already writes, so a tariff return is assembled from real counts rather than reconstructed from memory at year end.

What we will not automate

  • Age verification and refusal of service. A door decision is a legal one and it belongs to a certified person standing there.
  • Discipline. A variance report names a shift and a bar. It does not fire a bartender, and we will not build a system that acts on the number without a manager reading it first.
  • Sending to a guest list. Drafting is automatic, sending is not. A person reads the message and presses the button, every time.

A person stays in the loop

Anything that spends money, sends something irreversible, or carries a professional obligation arrives as a draft with a named reviewer. The system prepares the work. A person decides whether it ships.

You own the code and the data at the end of the engagement.

What the work taught us

  1. 01

    A variance report is only as honest as the item list under it. Two names for one bottle across two rooms, or a modifier nobody has priced in a year, and the subtraction is describing two different things. Cleaning the catalogue is the first real day of work and it is worth more than the software.

  2. 02

    A promoter paid per name will bring you names. A promoter paid on what the room spends will bring you spenders. The economics are set by what you can measure, and most venues can only measure heads, so most venues get heads.

  3. 03

    The queue is the first thing a guest buys. A scanner that stalls because the venue wifi died at 11pm costs more than any software licence, which is why the door has to work offline and read both the pass and the original ticket.

  4. 04

    A table minimum set too high leaves the best table in the room empty until one in the morning. Set too low and it goes to a party who order two bottles and hold it all night. The right number comes from what comparable tables actually cleared, not from what was printed last season.

  5. 05

    The catered crowd is the product. A room of people who are there for the same reason spends more, behaves better and comes back. A random room that happened to see an ad has no reason to return, and no campaign fixes that after the fact.

  6. 06

    The fastest bartender is not always the most profitable one. Speed shows up in the till and free pouring shows up in the count, and until you can see both against the same shift you are promoting on impression. That is why the loss data belongs in the scheduling module.
