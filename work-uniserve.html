Case study 01

Uniserve Communications: AI enablement for a Canadian ISP, two tracks in three months. In build

Gautam Lohia, Chairman and CEO, brought us in to AI-enable both the company and its own customers. Phase 1 mapped the opportunity and cut it to a shortlist. Phase 2 is in build now. Nothing on this page is presented as a finished result.

The situation

About 250 tickets a month, two ticketing systems, and a mandate from the owner.

Uniserve is a Canadian internet and telecom provider. Support handles about 250 tickets a month across ConnectWise and Halo. That volume is the whole reason the engagement exists.

The mandate came from the top, not from a department. Gautam Lohia asked for a three month AI enablement programme with two tracks running in parallel: one internal, for the people who run the company, and one external, for Uniserve's own customers. Most AI programmes pick one and quietly drop the other. This one was scoped as both from the first meeting.

Two ticketing systems is the detail that matters. When intake is split across ConnectWise and Halo, the same customer problem can arrive twice, get worked twice, and be counted twice. A technician spends part of the day answering a question that was already answered somewhere they cannot see. That is the leak the customer-facing track is aimed at.

Phase 1 did not start with a tool. It started with a map. Buying software before you know where the work repeats is how the 95 percent figure below happens.

Phase 1

The opportunity heatmap, then the cut.

Four functions read for repeated work. Eight to ten use cases written up with ROI next to each one. Three to five kept.

We built an AI opportunity heatmap across four functions: customer support, NOC and operations, sales and quoting, and finance and admin. Each function was read for three things: where work repeats, where information is re-typed from one system into another, and where a person waits on another person before they can finish.

  • 01

    Customer support

    About 250 tickets a month across two systems. The heaviest concentration of repeated questions, and the reason the customer-facing track exists.

  • 02

    NOC and operations

    Read for where an engineer waits on information that already exists somewhere in the estate.

  • 03

    Sales and quoting

    Every proposal starting from a blank page and a hunt through old files. This became the proposal assistant.

  • 04

    Finance and admin

    Read for re-typing between systems and for documents nobody can find twice.

Scoring came next. Each candidate use case was written up with the arithmetic beside it, so the ranking was arguable rather than asserted. The shortlist was then cut on ROI plus feasibility, in that order. Feasibility is not a footnote here: a use case that scores well but cannot be hosted in Canada is not a use case for Uniserve, and it was struck for that reason and no other.

Nothing was chosen because it demos well. That is the single biggest difference between a heatmap and a pitch.

Phase 2

What is being built now.

One customer-facing pair and three internal tools, all of them reading Uniserve's own tickets and documents rather than general web text.

The customer-facing piece is an AI chatbot and a voice agent for residential support. Both are trained on Uniserve's own tickets and documentation, which is what makes the answers specific to a Uniserve line on a Uniserve network instead of generically plausible. It is also what makes the 20 percent target below reachable: a customer who gets the real answer at 11pm does not create the ticket a technician reads at 9am.

The internal piece is three tools. A ticket summariser that reads the thread and drafts a response for an agent to edit and approve. A sales assistant that builds a proposal from the notes taken on the call. And a Uniserve Copilot that answers questions by retrieving from the company's own documents, so an answer comes with the document it came from.

One technician is the unit. Ten technicians is ten times the line. That is the whole point of writing the sum out instead of printing a headline: you can disagree with any row and recalculate it yourself.

Two of the inputs are Uniserve's own: the ticket volume and the hourly cost. Two are assumptions we are prepared to defend and to be measured on: the 20 percent deflection target and about 250 working days in a year. If the deflection lands at 10 percent, the line halves, and we would rather you knew which rows carry the risk than be handed a single confident number.

The constraints that shaped the build

Every byte stays in Canada, and a person approves anything irreversible.

Uniserve is a Canadian carrier, so all data and all models had to be hosted in Canada. That was fixed before the first line of code, and it ruled out a long list of otherwise obvious choices. It is also the reason the platform partner matters as much as the model does.

We build on Railtown AI: the RailTracks agent SDK for the agents themselves, the RailEngine data layer underneath them, and Conductor for observability, so there is a record of what an agent did and why. Observability is not a nice-to-have on a carrier's support desk. If an agent drafted a wrong answer, someone has to be able to go back and see how.

The third constraint is a rule rather than a technology. A human approves before anything irreversible sends. The summariser drafts and an agent approves. The proposal assistant drafts and a salesperson approves. The customer-facing chatbot answers within what it can retrieve and hands off rather than inventing. That gate costs a few seconds per ticket and it is not coming out.

How it is measured

Method

Redundant customer-created tickets counted before and after, out of the same ConnectWise and Halo intake. The clock starts at deployment, not at signature. If the system misses the agreed bar we keep working at no additional cost until it clears.
