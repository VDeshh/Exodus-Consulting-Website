A dealership holds one customer in six systems that never reconcile.

How it actually runs

Before anything is built, this is the week we are describing.

No software can be scoped from a category name. These are the specifics of automotive dealers and service that decide what is worth building and what is not.

  1. 01

    The phone goes to voicemail at eight

    Doors open and the service drive fills. Three advisors are writing up the cars standing in front of them, so the calls stack up behind. A customer asking whether their part came in gets voicemail, and most of them do not leave one. By ten the queue has cleared and nobody knows how many people gave up and rang the shop down the road.

  2. 02

    One customer sits in five systems

    The DMS holds the repair order. The CRM holds the lead from the website. The scheduler holds the appointment, the marketing tool holds the email address, and the accounting package holds the payment. The same person exists in all five with a different spelling and two phone numbers. When the owner asks how many customers the store actually has, five answers come back.

  3. 03

    The write-up decides whether the claim gets paid

    A warranty claim lives or dies on what the advisor typed at seven in the morning. Miss the complaint, cause and correction wording, the technician code or the mileage, and the manufacturer rejects it or claws it back at audit. The rejection arrives weeks later on a statement, long after the technician has been paid and the car has gone home.

  4. 04

    Reconditioning has no owner and no clock

    A trade comes in. It waits for a detail bay, then for a safety inspection, then for a part nobody ordered on the day it arrived. Each hand-off is a text message. The used vehicle manager knows the car is late but not which stage ate the week, and every extra day is depreciation the store pays for quietly.

  5. 05

    Declined work is written down and forgotten

    The inspection finds four items. The customer approves one and declines three because of the bill that month. Those three lines sit inside a closed repair order, and nothing brings them back. Six weeks later the same customer books the same job at a tyre shop, and the store never learns it lost work it had already found.

What we build

6 builds that pay for themselves here.

Each one states what it reads, what it does, and what a person still approves. Scope is agreed after the audit, one build at a time, against a baseline you sign.

Voice agent on the service line

The agent answers every call, including the ones during the morning rush. It reads the scheduler and repair order status, so it can book an appointment, confirm a part has landed, quote posted menu pricing and say when a car is ready. Anything else transfers to an advisor with the caller's name, vehicle and reason already on screen.

Measured by: Share of inbound calls answered inside three rings.

Website chatbot for the repetitive questions

It answers what the email queue is full of: opening hours, what a scheduled service covers, whether a recall applies to a given VIN, loaner and shuttle rules, tyre storage, and what to bring to an appointment. It reads the store's own published policies rather than guessing. Anything about a price or a diagnosis goes to a person.

Measured by: Front desk emails and calls handled per week.

One customer record across every system

We read the DMS, the CRM, the scheduler and the marketing list into one shape, match records on name, phone, email and VIN, and score every match for confidence. Weak joins stay visible instead of being merged quietly. A person reviews anything below the threshold before two records become one, and nothing is deleted from the source systems.

Measured by: Duplicate customer records left in the merged set.

Declined work that comes back

A nightly job reads closed repair orders for declined inspection lines, checks the vehicle has not since had the work done elsewhere in the group, and builds a follow-up list by job and by how long it has waited. The advisor sees the car, the item, the original estimate and the date, and approves the message before it sends.

Measured by: Dollars of declined work recovered each month.

Reconditioning clock from trade to line

Every used unit is stamped at each stage: arrival, appraisal, mechanical, body, detail, photos, listed. The stamps come from the shop system and a scan on a phone rather than a whiteboard. When a car sits longer than the rule for that stage, the manager gets one alert naming the car and the stage. Priority changes stay with the manager.

Measured by: Average days from trade acceptance to front line.

A daily report that cites its rows

One page each morning: repair orders written, hours sold per technician, effective labour rate, parts on back order, aged used units and calls answered. Every figure links back to the record it came from, so a number that looks wrong gets opened rather than argued about. Nothing is estimated, and if a source failed to load the report says so.

Measured by: Minutes spent building the Monday numbers by hand.

Ontario only, and it counts charges laid rather than convictions. Other provinces are regulated separately and publish differently.

Where the data comes from

Your systems of record stay exactly where they are.

We read them, we do not replace them. Each one below says what we connect to, how, and the line the build does not cross.

The dealer management system

Usually: PBS Systems, Serti, CDK Global, Reynolds and Reynolds, Quorum XSELLERATOR, Tekion, Dealertrack DMS

What it holds. Repair orders, technician time and flat rate hours, parts stock and orders, deal jackets, warranty claims, and the general ledger the accountant signs.

How we connect. Vendor API where the DMS opens one. Where it does not, a scheduled export or a read-only replica we build against, refreshed nightly.

Where it stops. Nothing we build posts, closes or edits a repair order, a claim or a ledger entry. The DMS stays the system of record and a person does the writing.

CRM and service scheduling

Usually: VinSolutions, Activix, DealerSocket, Elead, Xtime, Dealer-FX, Kimoby, myKaarma

What it holds. Leads, appointment slots and shop capacity, the message log with each customer, inspection results and the video the technician recorded.

How we connect. API for reads, plus a webhook on appointment created and appointment changed so the voice agent and the reports are never working from last night's copy.

Where it stops. No booking lands in a technician's bay outside the dispatch rules the shop foreman set. Capacity changes stay with the person who owns the schedule.

Shop management, parts and books

Usually: Protractor, Mitchell 1 Manager SE, Tekmetric, Shop-Ware, NAPA TRACS, Lankar, with QuickBooks or Sage behind them

What it holds. Estimates and repair orders, the parts catalogue and supplier pricing, labour times, technician efficiency, payables and receivables.

How we connect. Scheduled export or a read-only replica, matched against the parts catalogue so a variance points at a part number rather than a hunch.

Where it stops. We never place a parts order or change a sell price automatically. The system flags the shortfall and a person orders.

Built around your rules

The regimes that govern this work, and how the build answers each one.

Constraints come first, because they decide the architecture. Bring us your hosting, residency and regulatory rules at the start and we design to them rather than around them.

Regulatory and professional obligations that shape a build in automotive dealers and service.
Regime What it demands here How the build complies
PIPEDA, with BC PIPA, Alberta PIPA and Quebec Law 25 Collect only what the job needs, say why you are collecting it, keep it secure and hand it over when the customer asks. Quebec adds transparency duties where a decision about a person is made by automated means. Customer data stays in Canada where that is the requirement. Each build takes the fields it needs and no more, every read and write is logged, and no automated output affects a customer until a named person approves it.
CASL Every commercial electronic message needs consent, clear sender identification and a working unsubscribe. A purchase or a paid repair gives implied consent for a limited window, and that window expires. Consent basis and its expiry date sit on the customer record. The follow-up builder excludes anyone without a valid basis, stamps identification and unsubscribe into every send, and an advisor approves the list before it goes.
Provincial dealer regulators: OMVIC in Ontario, the Vehicle Sales Authority in British Columbia, AMVIC in Alberta Registration of the dealership and its salespeople, all-in price advertising, and disclosure of a vehicle's history and material condition before the customer commits. An advertised price is assembled from one priced record, so the figure on the website is the figure in the deal. Disclosure fields are mandatory on a listing and nothing publishes with them empty.
PCI-DSS Card data must not be stored or handled outside a compliant environment. That covers the cashier terminal, any pay-by-text link and the tablet on the service drive. We do not touch card numbers. Payment stays inside the processor the store already uses, and our systems hold a payment reference and a status, nothing more.

What we will not automate

  • We do not let software authorise a repair or change a price on a repair order. An advisor approves every dollar a customer is asked to pay, in their own name.
  • We do not let a bot give a safety opinion. A caller describing brakes, steering or a warning light is put through to a person on that call, not into a queue.

A person stays in the loop

Anything that spends money, sends something irreversible, or carries a professional obligation arrives as a draft with a named reviewer. The system prepares the work. A person decides whether it ships.

You own the code and the data at the end of the engagement.
